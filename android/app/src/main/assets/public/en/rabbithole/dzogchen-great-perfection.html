Dzogchen: The Great Perfection

Text by Vangelis Vadarma Petropoulos — republished with the author's permission.

The Great Perfection is considered by many Tibetans as the pinnacle of Buddhist spirituality and challenges the view that the human mind intrinsically exists independently of all conceptual frameworks. Shattering the core of our empirical identity, the practice of the Great Perfection delves deeply into the prevailing belief in the existence of an inherently true, locally defined, egocentric mind.

According to this tradition, the classical strategy for investigating the ontological status of the mind is the firsthand examination of the way mental events are created, unfold, and disappear, including consciousness itself. The fundamental challenge in this practice is the discrimination — through penetrating awareness — between what is provided sensorily and what is projected conceptually onto perceptual experience.

Within the context of this mode of meditative inquiry, the practitioner focuses attention on the appearances of mental events, closely observing the exact manner in which they emerge into the field of mental perception. Practically, meditative inquiry is guided by questions such as 'Do mental events appear all at once or gradually? Can their point of origin be identified?'. What is the nature of that from which these mental events emerge?

It has been said that thoughts and feelings emerge or are produced by the mind. What is sought now is the source of the 'mind' from which mental events appear to emerge. In the next phase of this inquiry, the practitioner meticulously examines the location of mental events. Once again, experience itself is left to answer this question without preconception.

Within the practice of the Great Perfection, a thorough firsthand examination is performed to locate mental events. This process is driven by questions like 'Are mental events located inside the body? If so, precisely where in the body is their presence experienced? If they are outside the body, in what specific part of the environment are they located? Does the awareness of mental events share the same location as these objects of awareness?'

Given that Buddhist introspectors consider mental events to appear 'within the mind', it naturally follows to rigorously examine the nature of the space of perception within which they appear. Ultimately, in this series of inquiries, the practitioner examines how mental contents disappear. Some Buddhist texts mention that they return to the Substratum Consciousness — or the Ground of Becoming — where they are stored as tendencies or latent impulses. And again, the practitioner seeks the true source of the phenomenon called 'mind'.

The essence of the Great Perfection practice for investigating the nature of the mind is perfectly recorded by Padmasambhava (an Indian Buddhist Master, 8th century): 'With the gaze of the mind steady, activate your awareness unwaveringly, clearly, resolutely, and unhindered, without directing it towards any meditative object, within the sphere of the mind...'

'...As the stabilization of attention increases, thoroughly examine the stability of consciousness. Then, gently relax and remain calm. Subsequently, with perseverance, continuity, and steadiness, observe the consciousness of the present moment. What is the nature of this mind? Let it immovably observe itself. Is it something lucid and still, or is it an unsubstantial emptiness? Is there anything you recognize?'

Through this kind of inquiry, Buddhist introspectors have concluded that the mind and awareness itself are inherently, by nature, objectively unidentifiable. As long as we consider them to be inherently existing things or events, it is impossible to locate them. The same applies to all sensory and conceptual contents of awareness.

The mind, like all other phenomena, is revealed to be empty, but not a non-existent nothingness. It is luminous, knowing, and empty, like boundless space, without center or periphery, flooded with transparent light. From this luminous space of non-local awareness, all phenomena emerge, always in relation to the conceptual frames of reference within which they are defined.

However, neither the objects of awareness nor the awareness of them exist independently of their conceptual designations. The recognition of this fundamental nature of the world of experience imparts a dreamlike character to life as a whole, within which all constructed divisions between subject and object, self and non-self, are eliminated.

The moment the practitioner realizes that the mind and mental contents are not inherently self-existent, they are ready to penetrate the nature of primordial consciousness, which transcends all conceptual constructs, including the concepts of existence and non-existence. This is the central theme of the Great Perfection practice and is considered the deepest insight.

Within the practice of the Great Perfection, deep attention is given to the spaces within which physical and mental phenomena emerge, abide, and disappear. At first glance, there seem to be two distinct types of spaces: The external space, within which we experience the environment, other people, and our own body.

And the internal space, within which we personally experience mental functions—namely thoughts, feelings, mental images, and dreams. According to Buddhist theory, all external, public events and all internal, personal events are equally 'physical', in the sense that their creation depends on prior causes and conditions.

The idea that only coarse, tangible matter and its properties are 'physical' objects, while anything immaterial is 'unnatural' or 'supernatural', is entirely foreign to the Buddhist understanding of the world. According to the Buddhist view, the physical world is filled with myriads of phenomena, many of which are composed of atoms along with their respective properties.

There are, however, phenomena that do not have such a structure. Such phenomena are, on one hand, consciousness along with its mental contents, and on the other hand, concepts such as justice, information, numbers, geometric shapes, the mathematical laws of nature, space, and time.

Buddhism rejects materialism, as well as the Cartesian mind-matter dualism. It might, perhaps, be considered a kind of empirical pluralism, which recognizes that the physical world is far richer and more mysterious than we think and does not lend itself to ontological categorizations.

The Great Perfection — in absolute agreement with the view of the Middle Way, upon which it is based — avoids assigning individual substance of any kind (mental, physical, or otherwise) to the contents of consciousness. The practice of the Great Perfection is based on the realization that everything in the universe, including the concept of nirvana, is empty of its own inherent existence.

Despite the fact that seemingly everything appears to exist independently, in reality, all forms do not exist outside of buddha-nature, which is identical to the absolute space of phenomena. All phenomena are like the reflection of stars on water or like rainbows in the sky. None of this exists objectively.

They are all manifestations of the absolute space of phenomena, which is primordially pristine and equally present in all aspects of reality. In the Great Perfection, buddha-nature is known as primordial consciousness, that is, the 'natural radiance' of the absolute space of phenomena, which manifests with the clarity and purity of dawn and the rising sun.

Dudjom Lingpa comments: 'It is not a blind and barren state of nothingness, an endless darkness born of ignorance... Just as heat is naturally present in the nature of fire... exactly so... there is a universal knowledge and full awareness of all phenomena, without being divided or differentiated into objects. Primordial consciousness is self-arising, naturally pure... It is the radiant, pure boundlessness of space that permeates everything, utterly undefiled'.

The realization of primordial consciousness, which is the mind of the Buddha, is the realization of your own nature, of your own brilliantly luminous mind, which is traditionally known as the 'clear light of awareness'. This primordial awareness is neither good nor bad, because it transcends all dualities and conceptual constructs.

It is the unalterable, primordially pure, all-pervading absolute space of reality. This ultimate ground transcends time as we know it, for it surpasses the past, the present, and the future. There are three inseparable aspects in the Great Perfection: primordial consciousness, the absolute space of phenomena, and the energy of primordial consciousness.

This third aspect is the life force that diffuses throughout the universe. It manifests clearly in all living beings and plants, while the awareness of all sentient beings stems from primordial consciousness. All other forms of matter and energy are derivatives of primordial consciousness. All relative manifestations of spacetime in the universe are expressions of the absolute space of phenomena.

The Dalai Lama expressively states: 'Every given state of consciousness is governed by the pure light of primordial awareness. No matter how solid ice is, it never loses its true nature, which is water. In the same way, any concept whatsoever is such that its basis, its ultimate fundamental state, lies within the sphere of primordial consciousness. Everything emerges from it and to it returns'.

Dudjom Lingpa explains that although the absolute space of phenomena... is present in the individual mental streams of all living beings, it is nevertheless strictly confined, or rather 'freezes' due to dualistic apprehension. When each person, as an observer-participant of our world, grasps objects as inherently real, self-existent, and independent of our consciousness, then they appear as external, solid, and concrete.

This resembles water, which, while in its natural liquid state, at some point freezes from the cold wind. Due to the dualistic function of the mind — through the subject/object dipole — the ground of the mind, which is naturally free, freezes and crystallizes, creating the forms of things.

Within the scope of the Great Perfection, the series of meditations begins with an initial, partial realization of emptiness, akin to the initial experience of a lucid dream, wherein you know that nothing appearing to your mind exists on its own, independently of your awareness of it.

Based on this first insightful glimpse, the practitioner continues to investigate the nature of all phenomena until realizing that everything is empty of its own existence. The culmination of this line of inquiry is the recognition of the 'one and single taste of great emptiness', the awareness that everything naturally arises from the domain of the absolute space of phenomena and has no existence outside this ultimate ground.

From the moment the practitioner reaches this realization, their mind fully assimilates it and never parts with it, whether during meditation or outside of it. They have been freed from the need to distinguish between ultimate reality and the phenomenal world. Now everything has the 'same taste', everything is governed by primordial purity and equality.

The moment you recognize the identity of the brilliantly luminous mind with the mind of the Buddha, your own consciousness is revealed to be an expression of primordial consciousness. Previously, as long as you were in ignorance and considered your egoic identity real, you merely saw primordial consciousness disguised as personal consciousness.

Dudjom Lingpa comments: 'The experience of the transformation of individual consciousness into primordial consciousness is like realizing that a scarecrow is what it is and not a human. Thus, the correct realization of the mode of existence of consciousness transforms it into primordial consciousness.

It is not necessary for consciousness to be lost in absolute space and for primordial consciousness to emerge in its place from somewhere else. On the contrary, let us not forget that such a thing seems to occur due to clinging to the idea of a separate self and the absence of identity.

Consciousness is that which produces the first flicker of knowledge through the presence of an object, like the reflections of stars appearing in the waters of the ocean. What arises is subject to the influence of conceptual consciousness, restricted by reification — the artificial construction of forms — and thus leads to delusion. Knowing the causes that create this illusion leads to the realization of primordial consciousness'.

The truth is that our mind was never separate from primordial buddhic consciousness. The impression to the contrary is due to the delusion that our perceptions, thoughts, and feelings are our property. In reality, none of them have an individual identity.

Experience shows that there is nothing within the mind that can be considered 'I' or 'mine'. But when we gaze at phenomena with our ordinary consciousness — which is under the influence of differentiation — the impression is created that they are separate from us, appearing in the form of objects, which we have reified and then perceived as inherently real and independent.

In this way, we drown in the delusion that obscures the true nature of our consciousness. By realizing how this delusion is created, we arrive at the realization of primordial consciousness.

According to the Great Perfection, the fundamental difference between the Buddha and the ordinary person is that the former knows who they are, while the latter does not. Consequently, the root of suffering is the attachment to the idea that what is not actually 'I' and 'mine' is 'I' and 'mine'. It is a state of primordial ignorance (spiritual blindness) concerning our true nature.
